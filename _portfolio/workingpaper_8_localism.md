summary: "When residents object to a proposed building, who is actually speaking &mdash; and from how far away? This project compares local and non-local opposition to housing in Vancouver across two very different channels: the in-person public hearing and the written online comment."
---

Opposition to new housing is usually described as a neighborhood phenomenon: the people who turn up to object are assumed to be the people who live next door. That assumption carries real weight in planning practice, because the standing of an objection often rests on proximity to the site.

This project tests the assumption directly. Using the public record generated by rezoning applications in the City of Vancouver, we identify where commenters are writing or speaking from, and ask how localism shapes the substance of what they say.

Two comparisons organize the work:

- **Local versus non-local.** How much of the opposition to a given project comes from the surrounding blocks, and how much from elsewhere in the city? Do near and distant commenters raise different concerns &mdash; shadowing, parking, and character close in; affordability, density, and citywide policy further out?
- **Hearing versus comment form.** The in-person public hearing and the written online comment are different instruments, with different costs of participation and different audiences. We compare the language and reasoning used in each to see whether the channel itself selects for particular voices and particular arguments.

The analysis combines large language models with structured coding of the hearing transcripts and comment records, so that each classified statement can be traced back to its source passage.
